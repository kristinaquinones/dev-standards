# Marketing Spec (Problem & Opportunity Spec)

## Overview

A marketing spec is a short, structured document that defines the problem or opportunity you are addressing before you commit to building a plan. It comes before the Marketing PRD, and it is the input that makes the PRD — and any AI you involve — significantly more useful.

The order matters:

**Spec → PRD → Brief**

Write the spec first. Use it to align on the problem and the bet. Then write the PRD to define requirements in detail. Then use the brief for execution.

## Why the spec comes first

Marketing teams often jump from a hunch or a directive straight into tactics: drafting copy, spinning up campaigns, briefing agencies, or prompting AI systems. The work happens, but the steering logic never gets defined.

That usually produces:

- More output, but not necessarily more impact
- Extra revisions caused by unclear direction
- Work that is difficult to evaluate after launch
- AI-generated plans that sound complete but are not grounded in the right inputs

A spec fixes this by forcing a moment of structured thinking before execution begins. It is not a full requirements document — that is the PRD's job. It is a fast, focused exercise to answer the most important upstream questions: what is the problem, who actually has it, why does it matter, and is it worth doing.

## The spec-driven marketing mindset

Marketers have products. Campaigns, launches, briefs, content programs — these are deliverables, and deliverables benefit from the same kind of structured thinking that engineers and product managers apply to their work.

That means:

- **Understanding the audience deeply**, not just naming a segment — understanding what they want to do, feel, or become, and what is getting in their way
- **Defining deliverables clearly**, including what is explicitly out of scope
- **Establishing success criteria up front**, so you can evaluate impact after launch

A spec makes this structured thinking fast and reusable. It also becomes your primary steering document when working with AI. A broad, unspecific request produces a broad, averaged-down response. A well-defined spec produces tighter plans, more relevant output, and significantly less back-and-forth refinement.

## Using AI with a spec

AI does not replace judgment. It compresses execution — which makes good input more valuable, not less.

LLMs are non-deterministic: the same open-ended prompt will not produce the same output twice. But the quality of the output is heavily influenced by the quality of the input. A spec gives the model enough structured context to produce something useful instead of something generic.

**Without a spec:** You get an averaged-down plan that reflects common patterns, not your specific audience, product, or moment.

**With a spec:** You get a plan grounded in defined purpose, specific audience insight, clear scope, and measurable outcomes.

**With a spec and a context-loaded model:** You get an even tighter plan, with clearer gap identification and suggestions that reflect your brand, constraints, and historical performance.

When briefing an AI system, provide the spec first. Ask the model to identify gaps or ambiguities before generating outputs. Review everything for drift, hallucinations, and filler that sounds right but adds no value.

## The five spec-driven marketing questions

A marketing spec must answer five questions. These answers do not need to be exhaustive — the PRD fills in the detail. They need to be honest and specific enough to align stakeholders and steer execution.

### 1. Purpose

What outcome are we driving, and what problem are we solving?

This is the "why this exists" question. It should name the specific change you want to create and the friction or gap you are addressing.

- What are we trying to make happen?
- What problem or friction are we addressing?
- Why does this matter now?

### 2. Impact

What is the scope of impact, and is it worth doing?

This is the bet. It separates meaningful opportunities from interesting but low-value work and makes the case for investing in this now.

- What happens if this works?
- Who or what is affected?
- Why are we willing to bet on this right now?

### 3. Audience

Who is this for, what do they want, and what are they trying to get done?

Audience understanding is the most important and most commonly skipped input. This goes beyond naming a persona. It should reflect what the audience actually wants to do, feel, or become — and what is getting in their way.

- Who specifically is this for?
- What do they want to do, feel, or become?
- What is getting in their way today?
- What would tell us we understand them well enough?

### 4. Deliverable

What are we shipping, and what are we deliberately not shipping?

Clear scope prevents drift. Define the type of work and what is explicitly out of scope so that teammates, partners, and AI systems can support execution without inventing major assumptions.

- What type of deliverable is this (campaign, launch, content, program)?
- What is in scope?
- What is explicitly out of scope?

### 5. Measurement

How will we know it worked, and when will we evaluate?

Success criteria should be defined before execution begins. If possible, use a SMART goal: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound.

- What is the primary success metric?
- What baseline are we comparing against?
- When will we review performance and decide what to do next?

## What comes after the spec

Once the spec is written and aligned on:

1. **Write the Marketing PRD** — use the spec as the foundation to fill in detailed requirements, constraints, audience depth, and a full measurement plan. See [Marketing PRD](marketing-prd.md).
2. **Use the brief for execution** — once the PRD is solid, turn it into a [Campaign Brief](templates/campaign-brief-template.md) to direct execution.

The spec is not the PRD. The PRD is not the brief. Each serves a different purpose at a different stage.

## Common pitfalls

- Writing the spec after the plan is already in motion (it becomes a rationalization document, not a steering document)
- Naming an audience segment without describing their actual motivations and friction
- Leaving the deliverable vague ("some content," "a campaign") rather than defining the specific type of work
- Skipping measurement because you are not sure what to measure (this is the time to figure that out, not after launch)
- Treating the spec as a formality rather than a real decision-making tool
